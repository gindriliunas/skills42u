Defibrillator (AED) Requirements for UK Workplaces: What Employers Actually Need to Know

There is no blanket UK law forcing every employer to buy a defibrillator — but the British Standard that insurers and HSE inspectors point to says most workplaces should have one anyway. Here's what's actually required, what's best practice, and how to work out which applies to you.

The Short Answer: Not a Law, Except for Schools

Under the Health and Safety (First Aid) Regulations 1981, there is currently no general legal duty for UK employers to install an automated external defibrillator (AED). The one confirmed exception is schools: since 2023 the Department for Education has required all state-funded schools in England to have at least one AED on the premises.

For every other employer — offices, warehouses, retail units, hospitality venues, construction sites — an AED is not mandated by name. What is mandated is a written first aid needs assessment, and that assessment is where the real answer to "do I need one?" gets decided.

What BS 8599 Actually Recommends

The British Standard BS 8599-1:2019+A1:2026 covers workplace first aid kits and equipment, and its updated guidance is specific: an AED should be available wherever 5 or more people are regularly present — including staff, visitors and customers — and it should be reachable within roughly a 2-to-3 minute round trip. In practice that means about one minute to locate the device and another minute or two to retrieve it and start using it.

BS 8599 is not law. But it is the recognised industry benchmark for "reasonable" first aid provision, and it's the standard insurers, auditors and HSE inspectors will measure you against if an incident occurs and no AED was available. Treat it as the practical minimum, not an optional extra.

Why Speed Matters More Than the Law

Survival from sudden cardiac arrest falls by roughly 10% for every minute that passes without defibrillation. The average ambulance response time for a Category 1 (life-threatening) call in England is around 8-9 minutes — long after the window for effective defibrillation has often closed. An on-site AED, used within the first few minutes, is frequently the single biggest factor in whether someone survives.

This is the real argument for having a device on site regardless of what the law strictly requires: by the time an ambulance arrives, an AED has usually already done its job or the opportunity has passed.

Do Staff Need Training to Use One?

No formal qualification is legally required. Modern AEDs are built for untrained bystanders — voice prompts talk the user through every step, and a built-in safety lock prevents a shock being delivered unless the device detects a genuinely shockable rhythm. You cannot use an AED to harm someone by mistake.

That said, hesitation costs seconds that matter. Every HSE-approved first aid course we deliver — EFAW, FAW, and Paediatric First Aid — includes hands-on AED practice on training devices, so staff who've held a unit before don't freeze when it's real.

How Many Devices Does a Larger Site Need?

The BS 8599 benchmark is response time, not headcount. Practical implications for common workplace types:

  • Single-floor office or shop: usually one AED near a central point such as reception is sufficient.
  • Multi-floor building: one device per floor, or one per fire escape stairwell, keeps every point within the round-trip target.
  • Warehouses & large sites: map device locations against travel distance, not square footage — a device at one end of a 200-metre warehouse does nothing for someone collapsing at the other end.
  • Construction sites: a portable AED should travel with the welfare unit or first aid point and move as the site progresses.

Folding This Into Your First Aid Needs Assessment

AED provision isn't a separate compliance exercise — it belongs inside your written first aid needs assessment under the 1981 Regulations. When you (or we, on your behalf) carry out that assessment, the questions that determine whether an AED is proportionate include:

  • How many people (staff, visitors, customers) are typically on site at once?
  • How far is the nearest ambulance station, and what's the realistic response time?
  • Does the workforce skew older, or include physically demanding roles with elevated cardiac risk?
  • Is the site large, multi-level, or spread across several buildings?
