First Aid Kit Requirements for UK Workplaces: The HSE Guide for Employers

There's no fixed legal checklist for what goes in a workplace first aid kit — the HSE requires provision to be "adequate and appropriate" instead. Here's what that actually means in practice, how BS 8599 sizes kits by headcount and risk, and what you're legally banned from including.

There's No Single Legal List — But There Is a Baseline

The Health and Safety (First Aid) Regulations 1981 deliberately don't prescribe an exact kit contents list. The legal wording is that first aid provision must be "adequate and appropriate to the circumstances" — meaning what belongs in your kit depends on your workforce, your hazards, and the outcome of your written needs assessment.

That said, HSE guidance and the British Standard BS 8599 describe a widely accepted baseline for a low-hazard workplace kit: a leaflet giving general first aid guidance, individually wrapped sterile plasters in assorted sizes, sterile eye pads, triangular bandages, safety pins, medium and large sterile wound dressings, and disposable gloves. Treat this as the floor, not the ceiling.

Sizing Your Kit: BS 8599's Tiers

BS 8599-1 scales kit size to headcount in a low-hazard environment:

Workforce Size Recommended Kit
Up to 25 peopleSmall workplace kit
25–100 peopleMedium workplace kit
Over 100 peopleLarge workplace kit (per 100, additional large kits)

Higher-hazard environments — construction, manufacturing, warehousing, food production with sharp tools or hot surfaces — should size up at least one tier from what headcount alone suggests, and supplement with hazard-specific items: burns dressings and burn gel for kitchens and industrial units, eye wash pods where chemicals or dust are present, and additional large wound dressings where lacerations or heavy manual handling injuries are a realistic risk.

What Must Never Be in the Kit

HSE guidance is explicit on this point: workplace first aid kits must not contain any medication — no paracetamol, aspirin, ibuprofen, antihistamines, or even over-the-counter remedies, regardless of how mild. First aiders are not trained or permitted to administer medication as part of their role. Keeping tablets in the kit creates a liability exposure that has nothing to do with genuine first aid provision, and a kit found with medication during an inspection is a compliance red flag, not a convenience.

If staff want to keep personal medication (such as their own paracetamol) at their desk, that's a matter for them individually — it should never be stored in or dispensed from the communal first aid kit.

Travel & Lone Worker Kits

If your needs assessment identifies staff who work off-site — delivery drivers, sales reps, engineers, care workers doing home visits, or anyone working alone away from a static base — a static wall-mounted kit in the office doesn't cover them. BS 8599-2 covers travel and vehicle first aid kits specifically: more compact than a static kit, designed to sit in a glovebox or bag, but built to the same core contents standard adjusted for a mobile environment.

Businesses with drivers or lone workers across construction, manufacturing or field-service roles should treat travel kits as a distinct line item in the needs assessment, not an afterthought.

Maintenance: Checking and Restocking

There's no legally fixed inspection interval, but best practice — and most written needs assessments — recommend:

  • Checking kit contents at least monthly against a printed inventory list
  • Restocking any used item immediately, not at the next scheduled check
  • Replacing items by their manufacturer expiry date, not just when visibly used or damaged
  • Appointing one named person responsible for kit maintenance across each site or floor
  • Logging kit checks alongside your fire extinguisher and other H&S inspection records for audit purposes

Kit Contents Are Only Half the Assessment

A well-stocked kit with no trained first aider to use it satisfies neither the letter nor the spirit of the 1981 Regulations. Kit provision and first aider numbers should come out of the same first aid needs assessment — see our guide on how many first aiders your business needs, and whether you should also consider an on-site defibrillator as part of the same review.
